312 · Rome, Roman Empire

Battle of the Milvian Bridge

Constantine I defeated his imperial rival Maxentius at the Tiber river to secure control over the Western Roman Empire.

Giulio Romano — Public domain · source
Who
Constantine I and his Roman army
What
Defeated Maxentius to rule the Western empire
Where
Milvian Bridge over the Tiber, Rome
When
28 October 312
What happened

Following the abdication of Diocletian on 1 May 305, rival claimants fought for authority across the Roman Empire. After Constantius died on 25 July 306, his troops in Eboracum proclaimed his son Constantine as Augustus. In Rome, Maxentius took the imperial title on 28 October 306. Although senior emperor Galerius recognized Constantine as Caesar, he treated Maxentius as a usurper and dispatched Severus to subdue him, though Severus was captured and executed after his troops defected.

By spring 312, Constantine marched into northern Italy with an army of 40,000 soldiers, defeating Maxentius's forces at Turin and Verona. On the night before the battle at the Milvian Bridge, Constantine experienced a vision of a light cross in the sky, prompting his troops to mark their shields with a Christian sign. On 28 October 312, Constantine engaged Maxentius outside Rome along the Tiber. Maxentius drowned during the battle, securing Constantine's victory.

What changed after

The day after the battle, imperial soldiers retrieved the body of Maxentius from the Tiber, decapitated him, and paraded his head through the streets of Rome before sending it to Africa. Constantine assumed control as the sole emperor of the Western Roman Empire.

In Rome, the Senate erected the Arch of Constantine to celebrate the victory, attributing the outcome to divine intervention. Eusebius of Caesarea and Lactantius recorded the engagement as the moment Constantine began his conversion to Christianity.

Southeast Asia
1928 · Batavia, Dutch East Indies

First Performance of Indonesia Raya

Wage Rudolf Supratman performed his newly composed patriotic song on violin before delegates attending a youth congress in Jakarta.

Who
Wage Rudolf Supratman
What
Performed the song Indonesia on violin
Where
Second Indonesian Youth Congress in Batavia
When
28 October 1928
What happened

In 1924, musician Wage Rudolf Supratman composed a piece titled "Indonesia" in Bandung after reading a magazine challenge asking for a national anthem. Inspired by Tan Malaka's 1926 book Massa Actie, Supratman revised the lyrics to reference shedding blood for the homeland. He finished the composition in 1928 as youth groups met to organize against Dutch colonial rule.

When Supratman attended the Second Indonesian Youth Congress in Jakarta on 28 October 1928 as a reporter, conference leader Sugondo Djojopuspito encouraged him to perform the song. Supratman played the piece on his violin before the assembly of delegates. To avoid suppression by Dutch colonial authorities, congress organizers kept news coverage quiet, and participants altered the chorus lyrics to "Mulia, Mulia!" during early renditions.

What changed after

The daily newspaper Soeloeh Ra'jat Indonesia published the song's lyrics on 7 November 1928, followed by Sin Po publishing the musical notation on 10 November 1928. Supratman retitled the piece "Indonesia Raya" in 1929 and sold 1,000 manuscript pamphlets. Record executive Tio Tek Hong produced commercial gramophone recordings, while Yo Kim Tjan brought home a copy from overseas before Dutch colonial authorities banned the song and seized unsold records in 1930.

Following the Proclamation of Indonesian Independence on 17 August 1945, the first stanza became the official national anthem. Government Regulation PP No. 44 of 1958 set its legal framework, and the second amendment to the 1945 Constitution codified it under Article 36B alongside Law No. 24 of 2009.
