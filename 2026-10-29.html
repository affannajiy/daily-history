1929 · New York City, United States

Black Tuesday Stock Market Crash

Panic selling on the New York Stock Exchange wiped out billions in share values during an unprecedented market collapse.

US-gov — Public domain · source
Who
Investors and New York Stock Exchange traders
What
Traded 16.4 million shares in panic selling
Where
New York Stock Exchange, United States
When
October 29, 1929
What happened

In September 1929, experienced stock market investors began selling their holdings as share prices stalled, initiating a downward trend on the New York Stock Exchange. On October 24, 1929, known as "Black Thursday", panic selling pushed daily trading volume to a record 12.9 million shares. In response, leading bankers formed a syndicate to purchase stock above market value to stabilize prices, echoing a strategy previously deployed during the Panic of 1907.

The intervention provided only a brief respite. On October 29, 1929, dubbed "Black Tuesday", heavy selling resumed, resulting in a record 16.4 million shares traded as investors rushed to liquidate their portfolios. Underlying economic problems, including agricultural overproduction and unsellable output among consumer goods manufacturers, exacerbated the rapid collapse of stock values.

What changed after

The United States Congress enacted several regulatory measures to reform financial markets, including the Banking Act of 1933, known as the Glass–Steagall Act, which separated commercial and investment banking. Congress also passed the Securities Act of 1933 and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, establishing the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission to enforce disclosure regulations and prohibit market manipulation, insider trading, and speculative practices.

Stock exchanges instituted mandatory halts in trading when share prices fall rapidly to limit panic selling. Financial markets continued their decline until July 8, 1932, by which time stocks had lost 90% of their pre-crash value, while the broader downturn persisted until the United States entered World War II on December 8, 1941.

Lion Air Flight 610 Crash

An Indonesian passenger flight crashed shortly after takeoff when erroneous sensor data triggered an undisclosed automatic flight control system.

Who
Lion Air flight crew and 181 passengers
What
Crashed into the Java Sea after takeoff
Where
Java Sea, off Karawang, Indonesia
When
October 29, 2018, 6:33 am local time
What happened

Lion Air Flight 610 departed Soekarno–Hatta International Airport in Jakarta at 6:20 am local time on October 29, 2018, bound for Depati Amir Airport in Pangkal Pinang. The Boeing 737 MAX 8 carrying 181 passengers and 8 crew members climbed to a maximum altitude of 5,000 feet before making erratic altitude fluctuations. Thirteen minutes into the flight, at 6:33 am, communication with air traffic control was lost, and the aircraft crashed into the Java Sea offshore from Karawang.

The investigation led by Indonesia's National Transportation Safety Committee revealed that a miscalibrated angle-of-attack sensor supplied by Xtra Aerospace transmitted incorrect data to the plane's Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System (MCAS). The system repeatedly forced the aircraft's nose downward. Boeing had omitted information about MCAS from flight manuals, leaving Captain Bhavye Suneja and First Officer Harvino unaware of how to stop the malfunction before impact.

What changed after

Following the crash, the United States Federal Aviation Administration and Boeing issued flight advisories reminding aircrews of standard runaway stabilizer procedures. After Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 crashed on March 10, 2019 due to similar MCAS malfunctions, aviation authorities worldwide grounded all Boeing 737 MAX aircraft.

Indonesia's National Transportation Safety Committee released its final investigation report on October 25, 2019, criticizing Boeing's design assumptions, the Federal Aviation Administration's certification procedures, and maintenance lapses at Lion Air and Xtra Aerospace. Boeing subsequently revised the software controlling the MCAS system before aviation regulators cleared the aircraft model to resume flights.
