What happened

The Council of Chalcedon opened its first session on October 8, 451, in the Bithynian city of Chalcedon. Convoked by the Roman emperor Marcian and attended by over 520 bishops or their representatives, it became the largest and best-documented assembly among the first seven ecumenical councils. The bishops gathered primarily to address ongoing Christological debates that had intensified after the death of Cyril of Alexandria in 444, following controversial doctrines promoted by an archimandrite named Eutyches.

During the council, the prelates evaluated key theological documents, including the Creed of Nicaea from 325, the Creed of Constantinople from 381, two letters by Cyril against Nestorius, and the Tome written by Pope Leo I. The assembly rejected both Nestorianism, which separated Christ's divine and human natures, and monophysitism, which viewed Christ as solely divine. To resolve these issues, the council formulated the Chalcedonian Definition, declaring that Jesus Christ is perfect in both deity and humanness.

What changed after

Disagreements over the council's rulings created a rift with the Oriental Orthodox Church, which rejected the proceedings and referred to the meeting as "Chalcedon, the Ominous." The Oriental Orthodox Churches subsequently schismed from the Chalcedonian churches.

In 544, the Church of the East ratified the council at the Synod of Mar Aba I. Later, in 1994, the Chalcedonian Catholic Church and the Nestorian Assyrian Church of the East signed the Common Christological Declaration, establishing that both institutions accepted the same doctrine of Christology.
