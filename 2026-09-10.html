Treaty of Saint-Germain-en-Laye

Allied powers and the Republic of German-Austria signed a post-war treaty dissolving the Austro-Hungarian Empire and reallocating its lands.

What happened

On October 21, 1918, 208 German-speaking delegates of the Austrian Imperial Council convened as a provisional national assembly at the Lower Austrian Landtag. Following the collapse of the Austro-Hungarian Army at the Battle of Vittorio Veneto, Karl Renner was elected German-Austrian State Chancellor on October 30. The Hungarian People's Republic terminated its real union with Austria on October 31, and the Armistice of Villa Giusti sealed the collapse on November 3. Emperor Charles I relinquished participation in the administration on November 11, and the assembly proclaimed German-Austria a democratic republic the next day, while new states including Czechoslovakia, Poland, and the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes formed across former imperial lands.

Italian forces occupied South Tyrol and Trentino, while Yugoslav troops entered Carinthia. After a constitutional assembly election on February 16, 1919, Renner was re-elected state chancellor and the assembly passed the Habsburg Law. Renner arrived in France in May 1919, where the Austrian delegation was excluded from negotiations led by French Prime Minister Georges Clemenceau. Facing an Allied ultimatum, Renner signed the Treaty of Saint-Germain-en-Laye at the Château de Saint-Germain-en-Laye on September 10, 1919.

What changed after

The treaty declared the Austro-Hungarian Empire dissolved and held Austria accountable for starting the war under Article 177. Cisleithanian Austria lost over 60 percent of its prewar imperial territory, and the Republic of Austria formally recognized the independence of Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Poland, and the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes.

The text established war reparations payable to the Allies, required the liquidation of the Austro-Hungarian Bank, and incorporated the Covenant of the League of Nations. Because the Covenant was included, the United States refused to ratify the treaty, later signing the US–Austrian Peace Treaty of 1921.

Ignacia del Espíritu Santo founded the first Catholic religious congregation for native Filipino women after overcoming colonial restrictions in Manila.

What they did

Ignacia del Espíritu Santo was born in Manila to María Jerónima, a Filipina, and José Yuco, a Christian Chinese migrant from Xiamen. Baptized on March 4, 1663, at the Church of the Holy Kings in the Parián de Chinos, she was expected by her parents to marry at age 21. Instead, she underwent the Spiritual Exercises under Jesuit priest Pablo Clain and chose to enter religious life, leaving her parents' home with only a needle and a pair of scissors.

To bypass Spanish colonial rules restricting native Filipinas from existing religious houses, Ignacia lived behind the Colegio Jesuita de Manila. Her lifestyle drew other laywomen, leading to the 1684 establishment of the Beatas de la Virgen María. In 1726, she authored religious constitutions for the group, which received formal approval from the Archdiocesan Chancery Office of Manila in 1732. She then resigned as mother superior, living as an ordinary member until her death at the altar rail of San Ignacio Church on September 10, 1748.

What still stands

The religious community she formed grew into the Congregation of the Sisters of the Religious of the Virgin Mary, which received a Decretum Laudis from Pope Pius X in 1907, papal approbation from Pope Pius XI in 1931, and final papal approbation from Pope Pius XII in 1948. Pope Benedict XVI declared her venerable on July 6, 2007.
