What happened

The Judiciary Act of 1789 was enacted on September 24, 1789, during the first session of the First United States Congress. Senator Richard Henry Lee reported the bill out of committee, Oliver Ellsworth authored it, and after debate in both houses the Senate and House approved the final version before President George Washington signed it into law.

The act established a federal judiciary, setting the Supreme Court at six justices—one chief and five associates—and created thirteen judicial districts with circuit and district courts. It also created the Office of Attorney General, United States attorneys and marshals for each district, and introduced removal jurisdiction for interstate lawsuits.

What changed after

The act gave the Supreme Court six seats and defined its original and appellate jurisdiction, replacing the earlier constitutional silence on court composition. It created a tiered system of circuit and district courts across thirteen districts, providing a national mechanism for enforcing federal statutes.

By establishing the Office of Attorney General and granting removal jurisdiction, the federal government gained direct legal representation and a tool to assert supremacy over state courts, reshaping the balance of judicial power in the new nation.
