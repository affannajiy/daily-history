Tim Berners-Lee Proposes the World Wide Web

English computer scientist Tim Berners-Lee laid the foundations for the World Wide Web by developing its protocol, server, and browser at CERN.

Liamd967 — CC BY-SA 4.0 · source
Who
Tim Berners-Lee
What
Implemented working World Wide Web system
Where
CERN, Geneva, Switzerland
When
1990
What happened

English computer scientist Tim Berners-Lee conceived a universal linked information system while working at CERN to solve the problem of storing, updating, and finding documents across a large organisation. Rejecting traditional tree structures and keyword tagging, he drew on concepts from his 1980 ENQUIRE system and Ted Nelson's 1965 hypertext model to design a network where documents linked across independent computers using embedded hyperlinks.

Berners-Lee submitted his initial proposal to CERN in March 1989. By the end of 1990, he had implemented a working system featuring the WorldWideWeb browser, an HTTP server at CERN, the original Hypertext Transfer Protocol (HTTP) specification, uniform resource locator (URL) syntax, and Hypertext Markup Language (HTML).

Timeline

1990 Berners-Lee implements a working web system, including the WorldWideWeb browser and an HTTP server.
January 1991 CERN releases the web technology to outside research institutions.
23 August 1991 The World Wide Web technology becomes available to the whole Internet.
30 April 1993 CERN makes the Web protocol and code available royalty-free.

What changed after

CERN released the web technology outside its research facility in January 1991 and opened it to the entire Internet on 23 August 1991. Within two years, creators established 50 websites. CERN made the underlying web protocols and code available royalty-free on 30 April 1993, allowing independent developers to build browsers and web software without licensing costs.

The launch of the Mosaic browser in 1993 and Netscape Navigator in 1994 accelerated adoption. Netscape held an initial public offering in 1995, triggering the dot-com bubble, while Microsoft responded by bundling Internet Explorer with the Windows operating system.

Seymour Hersh Exposes the My Lai Massacre

American investigative journalist Seymour Hersh broke the story of the My Lai massacre and its subsequent military cover-up in 1969.

What
Exposed the My Lai massacre and cover-up
Where
Washington, D.C., United States
When
1969
What happened

After working for United Press International and the Associated Press, Seymour Hersh resigned from AP's special investigative unit to work as a freelance reporter. In early 1968, he served as press secretary for anti-war Democratic presidential candidate Senator Eugene McCarthy before quitting ahead of the Wisconsin primary to return to freelance journalism on the Vietnam War.

In 1969, Hersh broke the story detailing the My Lai massacre committed by U.S. troops in South Vietnam and revealed efforts by military leadership to suppress information about the killings. Working independently without a staff position at a major newspaper, he published the investigative reports through a small news agency.

What changed after

Hersh received the 1970 Pulitzer Prize for International Reporting for uncovering the massacre. The disclosure brought public scrutiny to military operations in South Vietnam and led to national reporting on military conduct.

During the 1970s, Hersh joined The New York Times, where he reported on the Watergate scandal, secret U.S. bombings in Cambodia, and domestic surveillance programs conducted by the Central Intelligence Agency. He went on to win five George Polk Awards and two National Magazine Awards.
