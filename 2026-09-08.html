Division of Korea

United States troops moved to occupy southern Korea along the 38th parallel following Soviet military occupation of the peninsula's northern half.

Rishabh Tatiraju — CC BY-SA 3.0 · source
Who
United States and Soviet Union
What
Partitioned Korea into two occupation zones
Where
Korean peninsula
When
September 2, 1945
What happened

In the final days of World War II, the United States proposed dividing the Korean peninsula into two occupation zones along the 38th parallel. The Soviet Union accepted the proposal, leading to the declaration of General Order No. 1 and establishing a Soviet occupation zone in the north and a United States occupation zone in the south on 2 September 1945. This division was intended as a temporary arrangement until an international trusteeship could be established following decades of Japanese colonial rule, which had begun in 1910 when Emperor Gojong was deposed during annexation.

In December 1945, the Moscow Conference of Foreign Ministers reached an agreement on a five-year, four-power trusteeship for Korea. However, negotiations between the United States and the Soviet Union failed over the next two years due to Cold War tensions and Korean domestic opposition to the trusteeship framework. The United States subsequently referred the Korean question to the United Nations.

What changed after

In 1948, UN-supervised elections were conducted solely in the American-occupied southern zone. Syngman Rhee won the election, leading to the formal establishment of the Republic of Korea on 15 August 1948. In the northern zone, Kim Il Sung consolidated power, culminating in the declaration of the Democratic People's Republic of Korea on 10 July 1948, with both governments claiming total sovereignty over the peninsula.

On 25 June 1950, North Korean forces invaded South Korea in an effort to unify the country under communist control. The resulting Korean War concluded in 1953 in a stalemate, locking the division in place along the Korean Demilitarized Zone.

Gays in the Military

United States military regulations historically restricted gay service members through medical screening and psychiatric discharges prior to modern legal repeals.

Who
United States Armed Forces
What
Regulated and banned gay service members
Where
United States
When
1975
What happened

Military legal frameworks historically treated homosexual behavior as a criminal offense, with official prohibitions in the United States military dating back to a 1916 revision of the Articles of War. Recruitment procedures utilized physical examinations and personal interviews to identify recruits with effeminate physical traits, including wide hips or an absent gag reflex, which served as grounds for disqualification from enlistment.

Personnel accused of homosexual conduct were regularly discharged under military health classifications as sexual psychopaths, though the total volume of these military discharges decreased during periods of wartime mobilization. Institutional justifications for barring gay and lesbian personnel adapted over time, shifting from early claims regarding physical inability to concerns over operational effectiveness and inter-service conflict.

What changed after

Congressional hearings in the United States during 2010 focused on the Don't Ask Don't Tell policy, examining documented discrimination and arguments regarding military readiness. The policy was repealed later that year, making the United States one of the final developed nations to eliminate explicit prohibitions on openly gay, lesbian, and bisexual service members.

By the time of the repeal, more than 30 nations—including almost all members of NATO—allowed open military service for gay and lesbian citizens, while approximately 10 additional countries operated without explicit service bans.
