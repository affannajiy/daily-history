Inauguration of the Suez Canal

Ferdinand de Lesseps and the Compagnie de Suez opened an unlocked 193.3-kilometre sea-level waterway connecting the Mediterranean to the Red Sea.

NASA — Public domain · source

What happened

In 1858, French diplomat Ferdinand de Lesseps established the Compagnie de Suez to construct a shipping canal across the Isthmus of Suez, separating Africa and Asia. Designed by Alois Negrelli, the project was engineered as a single-lane, lock-free waterway allowing seawater to flow freely between the Mediterranean Sea and the Red Sea. Construction commenced in 1859, spanning across terrain from Port Said at its northern end to Port Tewfik at its southern terminus near Suez.

On November 17, 1869, the 193.3-kilometre (120.1-mile) canal officially opened for maritime navigation, incorporating passing locations at the Ballah Bypass and the Great Bitter Lake. By connecting the North Atlantic and northern Indian oceans via the Mediterranean and Red seas, the canal eliminated the need for vessels to circumnavigate the South Atlantic and southern Indian oceans.

What changed after

The opening of the Suez Canal shortened maritime trade routes between Europe and Asia, reducing the passage from the Arabian Sea to London by approximately 8,900 kilometres (5,500 miles). European shareholders, predominantly British and French, managed the concessionary operating company alongside the Egyptian government until President Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalised the waterway in 1956.

Under the terms of the Convention of Constantinople, the canal was designated for open use by commercial and military vessels of all nations during peace and war. Egypt managed operations through the state-owned Suez Canal Authority after nationalisation and later expanded the Ballah Bypass by 35 kilometres in 2016 at a cost of LE 59.4 billion.

Trial of Lieutenant William Calley

Lieutenant William Calley went on trial after U.S. Army soldiers murdered hundreds of unarmed South Vietnamese civilians in Sơn Mỹ village.

What
Placed on trial for the My Lai Massacre
Where
Quảng Ngãi Province, South Vietnam
When
1970
What happened

On March 16, 1968, U.S. Army soldiers from C Company, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry Regiment, 11th Brigade, commanded by Captain Ernest Medina, entered Mỹ Lai 4 in Sơn Mỹ village, Quảng Ngãi province. Expecting to encounter the Viet Cong's Local Force 48th Battalion, troops found no enemy forces but proceeded to search and round up unarmed villagers. Under the leadership of 1st Platoon leader Lieutenant William Calley Jr., soldiers executed civilians using automatic weapons, bayonets, and grenades, including a mass execution in an irrigation ditch. Warrant Officer Hugh Thompson Jr. and his helicopter crew attempted to intervene, while B Company killed additional villagers in nearby Mỹ Khê 4.

Initial Army reports classified the operation as a victory against Viet Cong fighters. The truth emerged in November 1969 when veteran Ronald Ridenhour and journalist Seymour Hersh exposed the deaths of between 347 and 504 civilians, including women, children, and elderly men. Twenty-six soldiers were ultimately charged with criminal offenses related to the war crime, leading to Calley's prosecution.

What changed after

Lieutenant William Calley Jr. was the sole individual convicted among the 26 soldiers charged for the crimes at Sơn Mỹ village. Found guilty of murdering 22 villagers, he was initially sentenced to life imprisonment, though his sentence was later commuted to three-and-a-half years under house arrest.

Public disclosure of the massacre sparked international outrage and intensified domestic American opposition to the Vietnam War. In response to the revelations, the Pentagon established the Vietnam War Crimes Working Group to investigate and verify additional incidents of war crimes committed during the conflict.
