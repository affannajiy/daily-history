Passage of United Nations General Assembly Resolution 2758

The United Nations General Assembly voted on October 25, 1971, to recognize the People's Republic of China as China's sole legitimate representative.

What happened

In 1945, the Republic of China joined the United Nations as a founding member and permanent member of its Security Council. Following the end of the Chinese Civil War in 1949, the Chinese Communist Party established the People's Republic of China on the mainland, while the Nationalist government retreated to Taiwan. Both governments adhered to a One-China policy, each claiming to be the sole legitimate government of China and opposing dual representation. Early attempts by the Soviet Union, India, and Albania to seat the People's Republic of China were repeatedly defeated in the General Assembly, where Resolution 1668 required a two-thirds majority for changes in member recognition.

Diplomatic momentum shifted in 1971 following a secret visit by United States official Henry Kissinger to meet Chinese Premier Zhou Enlai, facilitating negotiations between President Richard Nixon and Chairman Mao Zedong. On 25 October 1971, the General Assembly passed Resolution 2758, initiated by Albania and 16 other member states. Supported by communist nations, non-aligned countries, and several NATO members including the United Kingdom and Italy, the resolution expelled delegates of the Republic of China and seated the People's Republic of China on 15 November 1971.

What changed after

The People's Republic of China assumed China's permanent seat on the UN Security Council on 15 November 1971. President Richard Nixon visited mainland China the following year, initiating the normalization of China–United States relations. Deprived of its UN seat, the Republic of China was forced to enter international bodies under names such as 'Chinese Taipei'.

By September 2025, the People's Republic of China had exercised its Security Council veto twenty-one times, including blocking the admission of Bangladesh in 1972 and vetoing military observer deployments to Guatemala in 1997 and Macedonia in 1999.

2010 Mentawai earthquake and tsunami

A 7.8-magnitude earthquake off Sumatra's Mentawai Islands on October 25, 2010, triggered a destructive tsunami that killed hundreds of coastal residents.

What happened

On 25 October 2010, at 21:42 local time (14:42 UTC), a magnitude 7.8 earthquake struck off the western coast of Sumatra along the Sunda megathrust fault line. Centered approximately 150 miles west of Bengkulu near the remote Mentawai Islands, the quake ruptured an area measuring 220 by 120 kilometers at a focal depth eventually calculated at 12.8 miles. Although tremor intensity reached MM IV in Bengkulu, the most severe destruction occurred when the seabed movement generated a major localized tsunami.

Tsunami waves reached up to 3 meters in height and swept as far as 600 meters inland on South Pagai Island, while maximum run-up heights reached 16.86 meters on nearby Sibigau Island. Over 20 villages suffered severe damage or total destruction, displacing more than 20,000 residents across 4,000 households. The official death toll reached 435 people, with over 100 missing. Relief operations by the Indonesian military and international organizations faced severe delays until 27 October due to rough seas and adverse weather.

What changed after

The earthquake caused physical uplift along the coastal areas of South Pagai Island, enlarging existing landmasses and creating 6 new islands offshore. Emergency aid was distributed to 4,000 affected households after military transport ships cleared rough sea conditions on 27 October 2010.

What they did

Gerald Walter Robert Templer was born in Essex in 1898 and educated at Wellington College and Sandhurst before commissioning into the Royal Irish Fusiliers in 1916. He served on the Western Front during the First World War, participating in the Hundred Days Offensive after surviving a severe bout of diphtheria. During the Second World War, he held various senior command posts in the British Army. In 1952, following the assassination of Sir Henry Gurney by communist insurgents, Templer was appointed British High Commissioner in Malaya.

In Malaya, Templer directed operations against the Malayan National Liberation Army by expanding the Briggs Plan and combining food control with forced resettlements into guarded New Villages. He implemented counter-insurgency measures, including collective punishment, forced conscription, chemical defoliation, and cash bounties such as $250,000 for communist leader Chin Peng. After leaving Malaya in 1954, Templer served as Chief of the Imperial General Staff from 1955 to 1958, advising Prime Minister Anthony Eden during the 1956 Suez Crisis.

What still stands

Institutions and landmarks established by or named after Gerald Templer include the National Army Museum in the United Kingdom, Dewan Tun Templer at the Royal Military College in Sungai Besi, and Templer Park (Hutan Lipur Templer) in Rawang.
