1962 · Washington, D.C., United States

Cuban Missile Crisis begins

American reconnaissance flights over Cuba revealed Soviet nuclear installations, triggering a tense thirteen-day standoff between superpowers.

USAF — Public domain · source
Who
U.S. President John F. Kennedy and advisers
What
Informed of photographic evidence of Soviet nuclear missiles
Where
Washington, D.C.
When
October 16, 1962
What happened

U.S. President John F. Kennedy was officially informed of photographic evidence captured by a U-2 spy plane showing medium- and long-range nuclear missile launch facilities under construction in Cuba. In response, Kennedy convened the Executive Committee of the National Security Council to evaluate potential military actions. Advisers initially recommended an air strike on Cuban soil followed by an invasion, but Kennedy ultimately opted for a naval blockade, termed a quarantine, to prevent additional military supplies from reaching the island.

The confrontation stemmed from prior deployments, including U.S. Jupiter missiles in Italy and Turkey and Thor missiles in England, alongside American attempts to overthrow Fidel Castro's government. Seeking to deter a future U.S. invasion and solidify ties, Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev and Castro agreed in July 1962 to place Soviet nuclear weapons in Cuba. The resulting crisis brought the United States and the Soviet Union to the brink of full-scale nuclear war before diplomatic channels produced an agreement.

What changed after

The crisis concluded when the Soviet Union agreed to dismantle offensive weapons in Cuba under United Nations verification, while the United States made a public declaration not to invade Cuba and secretly agreed to remove missiles from Turkey. The resolution led to the establishment of the Moscow–Washington hotline to facilitate direct communication between the two superpowers. Khrushchev's concessions contributed to his later fall from power, as the outcome was viewed within the Soviet leadership as a humiliation.

Southeast Asia
1975 · Balibo, Portuguese Timor

Balibo Five killed in Portuguese Timor

Five television journalists covering incursions ahead of an invasion were murdered by military forces in East Timor.

Who
Indonesian military forces
What
Killed five foreign journalists reporting in a border town
Where
Balibo, Portuguese Timor
When
October 16, 1975
What happened

Five television journalists working for Australian commercial networks—comprising two Australians, two British citizens, and one New Zealander—were stationed in the town of Balibo to report on military incursions preceding an invasion. Aware that Indonesian troops were preparing an attack as part of Operation Flamboyan, the men believed their status as journalists would protect them, with reporter Greg Shackleton painting an Australian flag and the word 'AUSTRALIA' on a wall.

On October 16, 1975, Indonesian forces attacked the town and killed all five men. According to eyewitness and scholarly accounts, the journalists were unarmed, wore civilian clothes, and attempted to surrender before being executed. Their bodies were subsequently dressed in military uniforms with guns placed beside them in an attempt to portray them as combatants.

What changed after

The killings prompted decades of advocacy by families and colleagues seeking accountability, leading to an official 2007 Australian coroner's inquest that ruled the deaths were deliberate killings by special forces. The Balibo House Trust was established in honor of the victims to carry out charitable work in East Timor. Subsequent investigations, including a 2009 Australian Federal Police war crimes inquiry, closed without bringing criminal charges.
