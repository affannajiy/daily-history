1973 OAPEC Oil Embargo

Arab oil-producing nations cut production and banned petroleum exports to Western nations that supported Israel during the Yom Kippur War.

Who
Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries
What
Imposed a total oil embargo
Where
Middle East
When
October 16, 1973
What happened

In October 1973, the Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries (OAPEC) announced a total oil embargo against countries that had supported Israel in the Yom Kippur War. Led by King Faisal of Saudi Arabia, OAPEC initially targeted Canada, Japan, the Netherlands, the United Kingdom, and the United States, after US President Richard Nixon requested $2.2 billion to support Israel's military effort. The target list was later expanded to include Portugal, Rhodesia, and South Africa.

The embargo occurred as American domestic oil production was declining, having peaked in 1970 and fallen to 16% of global output by 1973. Combined with President Nixon's 1971 price ceilings on oil and growing reliance on imports—which had risen 52% between 1969 and 1972—Western economies were vulnerable to a direct reduction in Middle Eastern crude supplies.

What changed after

When OAPEC lifted the embargo in March 1974, global oil prices had quadrupled from $3 per barrel to nearly $12 per barrel globally. Prices in the United States reached even higher levels than the global average.

The embargo created the first global oil shock and brought an end to the era of low-cost energy that had fueled Western economic growth since 1945. A second oil shock followed in 1979 in the aftermath of the Iranian Revolution.

Completion of the Burma Railway

Imperial Japanese forces completed a 415-kilometer railway connecting Thailand and Burma through the forced labor of Asian civilians and Allied prisoners.

Completed the 415-kilometer Burma Railway
Where
Konkoita, Thailand
When

What happened

In June 1942, Imperial Japanese forces began planning a 415-kilometer railway connecting Ban Pong, Thailand, with Thanbyuzayat, Burma. Following Japan's defeat at the Battle of Midway in June 1942, sea routes carrying troops and supplies around the Malay Peninsula were vulnerable to Allied submarine attacks. Full-scale construction began on September 16, 1942, routing the track through Three Pagodas Pass and the Khwae Noi river valley.

On October 17, 1943, forced laborers working south from Burma met teams working north from Thailand at kilometre 263 near Konkoita. Officially named the Tai–Men Rensetsu Tetsudō by Japan, the line was ceremonially opened on October 25, 1943, when General Eiguma Ishida drove a copper spike into the track. To complete the project, Japan forced between 180,000 and 250,000 Southeast Asian civilians and over 61,000 Allied prisoners of war into hard labor.

What changed after

After World War II ended, most of the 415-kilometer railway was dismantled in 1947. A 130-kilometer section in Thailand between Nong Pla Duk and Nam Tok was reopened in 1957 and remains in operation.

In the 1951 Treaty of San Francisco, Japan provided official reparations to the Burmese and Indonesian governments, and the United Kingdom paid compensation to 60,000 Allied prisoners of war. No compensation or reparations were provided to the surviving Southeast Asian civilian laborers.
