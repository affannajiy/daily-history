Ratification of the Louisiana Purchase

President Thomas Jefferson and Secretary of State James Madison persuaded Congress to ratify the purchase of 828,000 square miles from France.

William Morris — CC BY-SA 4.0 · source
Who
President Thomas Jefferson and Congress
What
Ratified the Louisiana Purchase treaty
Where
Washington, D.C., United States
When
October 20, 1803
What happened

President Thomas Jefferson sought control of the Mississippi River port of New Orleans to secure free transit for American trade. Jefferson dispatched James Monroe and Robert R. Livingston to negotiate the purchase of New Orleans with French Treasury Minister François Barbé-Marbois. Prompted by the failure to suppress a revolt in Saint-Domingue and the prospect of renewed warfare with the United Kingdom, Napoleon Bonaparte offered to sell the entire Louisiana territory. The U.S. representatives agreed to acquire the land for $15 million.

Facing political opposition from the Federalist Party, Jefferson and Secretary of State James Madison urged Congress to ratify and fund the acquisition. The transaction encompassed 828,000 square miles of land in the Mississippi River drainage basin west of the river. Spain formally transferred power over the territory to France on November 30, 1803, preceding the formal U.S. takeover on December 20, 1803.

What changed after

The purchase extended United States sovereignty across the Mississippi River and nearly doubled the nominal size of the country, adding land that now spans fifteen U.S. states and two Canadian provinces. The acquisition encompasses all of present-day Arkansas, Missouri, Iowa, Oklahoma, Kansas, and Nebraska, alongside portions of nine other states and small parts of Alberta and Saskatchewan.

The western boundaries of the territory were subsequently defined by the 1819 Adams–Onís Treaty with Spain, while northern borders were adjusted under the Treaty of 1818 with Great Britain.

Southeast Asia
1944 · Leyte, Philippines

Landing at Leyte

General Douglas MacArthur led an amphibious assault on Leyte island, fulfilling his pledge to return and liberate the Philippines.

Who
General Douglas MacArthur and Allied forces
What
Landed on Leyte island
Where
Leyte, Philippines
When
October 20, 1944
What happened

In 1942, Imperial Japanese forces conquered the Philippines, prompting President Franklin D. Roosevelt to order General Douglas MacArthur to evacuate to Australia. MacArthur vowed to return to liberate the archipelago. In July 1944, Roosevelt met with MacArthur and Admiral Chester Nimitz in Hawaii to finalize plans for invading the Philippines. Following successful strikes by Admiral William F. Halsey's carrier aircraft during the summer of 1944, the invasion date for Leyte was moved forward to October under Operation King Two.

Allied landings began on October 17, 1944, under MacArthur's overall command. The invasion force included Lieutenant General Walter Krueger's U.S. Sixth Army, numbering approximately 202,500 personnel, supported by Vice Admiral Thomas C. Kinkaid's U.S. Seventh Fleet. Landing on Leyte's east coast, Allied troops engaged Lieutenant General Shiro Makino's 16th Division and fought against General Tomoyuki Yamashita's broader Japanese forces across Leyte Valley and the island's mountainous interior.

What changed after

The invasion ended nearly three years of Japanese occupation on Leyte and established land air bases for subsequent military operations across the Pacific Theater. Allied control of Leyte severed Japanese maritime transport routes to Borneo and Sumatra, disrupting petroleum and rubber shipments to Japan.

The capture of Leyte enabled Allied forces to initiate operations across the remainder of the Philippine archipelago, leading to the invasion of Luzon in early 1945.
