Women's March on Versailles

What happened

On the morning of 5 October 1789 women in the Paris marketplaces began rioting over the high price of bread. Their unrest quickly merged with revolutionary agitators, swelling the crowd to thousands. The demonstrators ransacked the city armory for weapons, then marched the roughly 21 kilometres (13 mi) to the Palace of Versailles, where they besieged the royal residence and pressed their demands on King Louis XVI.

The following day, 6 October 1789, the crowd forced the king and his family to leave Versailles and accompany them back to Paris. The National Constituent Assembly also relocated to the capital. These actions ended the king’s independent authority and shifted the balance of power toward the Third Estate, weakening the privileged orders of the French nobility.

What changed after

After 6 October 1789 King Louis XVI and the royal household took up residence in Paris, ending the independence of the monarchy. The National Constituent Assembly also moved from Versailles to the capital, concentrating political power in the city and giving the Third Estate a decisive voice in governance.

The march signaled a new balance of power that would ultimately displace the privileged orders of the French nobility, accelerating the revolutionary reforms enacted by the Assembly and paving the way for further constitutional changes.
