1945 · Tokyo, Japan

Formal Surrender of the Empire of Japan

The Empire of Japan surrendered unconditionally to Allied forces in 1945, bringing an end to the Pacific War and its imperial expansion.

What happened

After military successes in the Second Sino-Japanese War and the Pacific War—including the attack on Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941—Imperial Japanese forces were forced onto the defensive following Allied advances at Midway Atoll and Guadalcanal. The American island-hopping campaign captured Iwo Jima and Okinawa Island, leaving mainland Japan without adequate naval defense.

Facing a two-front war due to the Soviet invasion of Manchuria and two atomic bombs dropped by the United States on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, Japan accepted unconditional surrender under the Potsdam Declaration on 14 August 1945. On 15 August 1945, the Japanese Emperor broadcast the surrender on the radio, and the formalized surrender was issued on 2 September 1945, officially ending the Pacific War.

What changed after

Following the surrender, the territory of the Empire of Japan shrank to mainland Japan. United States military commander Douglas MacArthur administered the country during the Allied occupation, during which the Imperial Japanese Armed Forces were dissolved before being replaced by the Japan Self-Defense Forces in 1954.

A new Japanese constitution was enacted on 3 May 1947, officially ending the Japanese Empire and forming modern Japan. Allied reconstruction continued until 1952, when the Treaty of San Francisco took effect, while Okinawa was returned to Japan in 1972.

Death of U Ottama in Prison

Burmese national hero U Ottama died in prison in 1939 following a hunger strike against the British colonial administration.

What happened

Hunger striking developed as a method of non-violent political resistance where participants fast to demand policy changes or protest injustice. Strikers fast to exert pressure on custodial entities, refusing solid food while sometimes continuing to take fluids. When state authorities detain political protesters, custodial officials frequently respond with forced feedings or legislative releases.

Under British rule in South Asia, colonial authorities moved against public fasting fasts used to demand justice, abolishing the practice in India in 1861. In subsequent decades, political activists used hunger strikes while imprisoned by British authorities, including Mahatma Gandhi, who was jailed in 1922, 1930, 1933, and 1942.

What changed after

The use of prison hunger strikes forced custodial authorities to navigate bioethical dilemmas surrounding forced feeding and detainee health. Medical documentation showed that human bodies enter ketosis after three days of fasting, leading to severe organ and muscle breakdown when fat reserves are exhausted.

To manage hunger strikers without creating political martyrs, the British Parliament passed the Prisoners (Temporary Discharge for Ill Health) Act 1913, allowing authorities to release sick strikers temporarily and re-arrest them once recovered.
