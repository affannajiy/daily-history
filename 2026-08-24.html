Visigoths Sack Rome

Visigothic forces led by King Alaric I initiated the sack of Rome after years of alternating conflict and alliance with Roman authorities.

What happened

The Visigoths were a Gothic people who emerged in the Balkans during late antiquity, descending from the Thervingi who crossed into the Roman Empire in 376. After defeating Roman forces at the Battle of Adrianople in 378, they were unified under Alaric I, who ruled from 395 to 410. Alaric led his forces in military campaigns that alternated between fighting against Roman armies and establishing strategic alliances with the empire.

Tensions culminated in 410 when King Alaric I led his troops into Rome and began pillaging the city. The assault marked the culmination of years of political and military friction between Alaric's forces and imperial administrators.

What changed after

Following Alaric's campaign, the Visigoths were settled as foederati in southern Gaul in 418, establishing a kingdom headquartered at Toulouse before expanding into Hispania to displace the Suebi and Vandals. After their defeat by Clovis I at the Battle of Vouillé in 507, Visigothic rule consolidated across Spain and Portugal.

King Reccared I converted the monarchy from Arian Christianity to Nicene Christianity at the Third Council of Toledo in 589. The Visigothic Code of 654 later legally unified Goths and Romans until the kingdom collapsed following defeat by an Umayyad army at the Battle of Guadalete in 711.

U.S. Issues Cable 243 During the South Vietnamese Buddhist Crisis

The United States Department of State sent Cable 243 to Ambassador Henry Cabot Lodge Jr., signaling non-interference in plans to overthrow President Ngô Đình Diệm.

Who
United States Department of State
What
Cabled authorization permitting non-interference in coup planning
Where
Saigon, South Vietnam
When
August 24, 1963
What happened

Following a violent crackdown on Buddhist protests by South Vietnamese President Ngô Đình Diệm, the Kennedy administration reached a conviction that Diệm could not unite the country against communist forces. The United States Department of State dispatched Cable 243 to U.S. Ambassador Henry Cabot Lodge Jr., outlining that the United States would not attempt to stop Army of the Republic of Vietnam officers if they launched a coup to remove Diệm and his brother Ngô Đình Nhu.

Central Intelligence Agency officer Lucien Conein served as the liaison between the U.S. Embassy and the military plotters. Conein informed the generals that the U.S. would not intervene to halt their plan, and he supplied 3 million Vietnamese piastres, equivalent to approximately $42,000 USD, to reward military units that joined the rebellion.

What changed after

General Dương Văn Minh initiated the coup on November 1, 1963, capturing loyalist commanders and suffering few casualties. On November 2, 1963, Ngô Đình Diệm and Ngô Đình Nhu were captured and assassinated.

The coup dismantled the government of the Personalist Labor Revolutionary Party of the Republic of Vietnam. South Vietnamese citizens subsequently referred to the overthrow as the November 1, 1963 Revolution.
