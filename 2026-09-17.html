Signing of the United States Constitution

Thirty-nine delegates signed the new framework for the American federal government at Independence Hall, concluding four months of deliberations.

What happened

On September 17, 1787, thirty-nine delegates gathered at Independence Hall in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, to sign the final draft of the United States Constitution. Engrossed by Jacob Shallus, the document laid out the framework of the national federal government and its three branches: legislative, executive, and judicial. Twelve of the thirteen states sent representatives to the four-month convention, while Rhode Island refused to participate. Of the seventy-four chosen delegates, fifty-five attended, though several departed before the signing session, and three remaining delegates refused to sign.

The concluding endorsement, drafted by Gouverneur Morris and presented by Benjamin Franklin, used deliberately flexible language to encourage unanimous state participation. Convention secretary William Jackson signed a note authenticating four handwritten amendments to the final text. The signers ranged in age from twenty-six-year-old Jonathan Dayton to eighty-one-year-old Benjamin Franklin. Six signers had previously signed the Declaration of Independence, and thirty had served on active military duty during the American Revolution.

What changed after

The signing brought the four-month Constitutional Convention to a close and submitted the seven-article framework to the states for approval. Supporters sought to build momentum for the state ratifications required to make the federal government structure operational across the thirteen states.

Battle of Mabitac

Filipino forces commanded by General Juan Cailles defeated an advancing United States Army column along a flooded causeway in Laguna.

Who
Filipino forces under General Juan Cailles
What
Defeated US forces under Benjamin F. Cheatham Jr.
Where
Mabitac, Laguna, Philippines
When
September 17, 1900, 6:00 AM
What happened

On September 17, 1900, Filipino soldiers under General Juan Cailles defended trenches across a narrow causeway connecting Mabitac to Siniloan against an advancing United States force under Colonel Benjamin F. Cheatham Jr. Flooding left waist-deep water on the causeway and deeper water in surrounding rice fields, preventing U.S. troops from deploying off the road. Eight U.S. scouts sent ahead from the 37th and 15th Infantry Regiments were killed within fifty yards of the Filipino line, including Second Lieutenant George Cooper.

The main American body remained pinned down in mud several hundred yards away during a ninety-minute firefight. Captain John E. Moran attempted to rally the troops, while a U.S. Navy gunboat 1,300 yards away and a flank attack by sixty Americans failed to break the Filipino positions. Cheatham ordered a retreat back toward Siniloan. Cailles permitted Cheatham to retrieve the bodies of the eight dead U.S. scouts before executing a tactical withdrawal that moved his entire force to safety by the following day.

What changed after

Brigadier General Arthur MacArthur Jr. characterized the thirty-three percent loss of American operational strength as a profoundly impressive loss. General Juan Cailles evacuated his troops from Mabitac to central headquarters, allowing American forces to occupy the town without further resistance later on September 17, 1900.
