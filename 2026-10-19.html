1781 · Yorktown, Virginia, United States

Siege of Yorktown

Franco-American land and naval forces trapped British troops under General Cornwallis at Yorktown, compelling a capitulation that ended major land battles.

John Trumbull — Public domain · source
Who
Franco-American forces led by George Washington
What
Besieged and captured British forces at Yorktown
Where
Yorktown, Virginia
When
October 19, 1781
What happened

In the summer of 1781, French and American armies united north of New York City and decided, on the advice of Comte de Rochambeau, to move against British Lieutenant General Charles Cornwallis at Yorktown, Virginia. Cornwallis had been ordered to construct a defensible deep-water port at Yorktown and was shadowed by Continental forces under the Marquis de Lafayette. Meanwhile, French Admiral Comte de Grasse sailed his fleet from the West Indies to the Chesapeake Bay, where he established a naval blockade and defeated a British relief fleet under Sir Thomas Graves at the Battle of the Chesapeake, cutting off Cornwallis from sea escape and reinforcements.

George Washington and Rochambeau arrived at Yorktown on September 28, 1781, completely surrounding the British defenses. Allied forces constructed their first parallel and initiated a heavy artillery bombardment. On October 14, two assault columns—a French unit led by Vicomte de Deux-Ponts taking Redoubt No. 9 and an American unit led by Lieutenant Colonel Alexander Hamilton taking Redoubt No. 10—captured key British outer earthworks. This enabled the completion of a second parallel closer to British lines. Facing intense bombardment, Cornwallis requested terms on October 17, and the formal surrender occurred on October 19.

What changed after

The surrender of over 7,000 British troops at Yorktown led directly to peace negotiations between Great Britain and the United States. These talks culminated in the signing of the Treaty of Paris of 1783, formally recognizing American independence and concluding the Revolutionary War.

United States landing on Leyte

American military forces launched an amphibious invasion on Leyte island to liberate the Philippine Archipelago from Japanese army rule.

Who
US Sixth Army and Filipino guerrillas
What
Launched an amphibious invasion of Leyte
Where
Leyte, Philippines
When
October 17, 1944
What happened

In July 1944, President Franklin D. Roosevelt met with General Douglas MacArthur and Admiral Chester Nimitz in Hawaii to approve the invasion of the Philippines. Operating under the code name King Two, the invasion aimed to reclaim the archipelago from Imperial Japanese forces led by General Tomoyuki Yamashita. Following successful carrier strikes by Admiral William F. Halsey's 3rd Fleet that revealed weak Japanese air defenses, MacArthur's invasion date for Leyte was moved forward to October 1944.

Naval and land elements of the US Sixth Army under Lieutenant General Walter Krueger, totaling approximately 202,500 personnel, spearheaded the amphibious assault alongside local Filipino guerrilla units. Japanese forces on Leyte, primarily the 16th Division commanded by Lieutenant General Shiro Makino under the 35th Army, numbered roughly 20,000 troops. Allied troops landed across the sandy beaches and deep-water approaches of Leyte Valley to gain control of Highway 1 and secure suitable ground for military airfields.

What changed after

The landing established an Allied foothold in the central Philippines, enabling the Fifth Air Force and Thirteenth Air Force to operate from local airfields. Stripping Japan of direct control over sea lanes to Borneo and Sumatra disrupted shipments of vital resources like petroleum and rubber to Japan.
