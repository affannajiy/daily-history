Iraq invades Iran

Iraq launched a surprise attack across the Shatt al‑Arab, aiming to seize Iranian territory and curb revolutionary influence.

What happened

The Iraqi government, under President Saddam Hussein, opened hostilities by crossing the Shatt al‑Arab in September 1980, marking the start of the Iran–Iraq War. Their stated aims were to prevent the export of Ruhollah Khomeini’s revolutionary ideology, to suppress potential Shia unrest within Iraq, and to replace Iran as the dominant power in the Persian Gulf.

Initially expecting a swift victory, Iraqi forces made limited advances before their offensive stalled by December 1980, after which Iran began to regroup and later recaptured its lost territory.

What changed after

The United Nations brokered a cease‑fire that took effect on 20 August 1988, ending active hostilities without altering the pre‑war border. Both Iraq and Iran suffered severe financial losses and received no war reparations, leading to prolonged low‑level clashes until Saddam Hussein’s overthrow in 2003.

What they did

Prince Nguyễn Phúc Hồng Nhậm ascended the throne in 1847 as Emperor Tự Đức, becoming the fourth Nguyễn dynasty ruler and the last pre‑colonial monarch of Vietnam. His reign, lasting 36 years, was marked by staunch Confucianism, suppression of Christian missionaries, and a refusal to modernize or engage with foreign powers.

In the summer of 1849, a cholera epidemic devastated Vietnam and Cambodia, killing an estimated 600,000 people according to royal archives. Tự Đức also faced internal challenges, including the rebellion of his half‑brother Hồng Bảo, who was arrested in 1854 and died in prison, and the later uprising of Hồng Bảo’s son Đinh Đạo in 1866, which Tự Đức crushed, ordering the execution of the rebels and their families.
