Sack of Rome

The Visigoths under King Alaric completed their three-day sacking of Rome in August 410, marking the city's first fall to foreign enemies in centuries.

What happened

The Goths entered the Roman Empire in 376 when the Huns forced Therving Goths under Fritigern and Alavivus to seek refuge in the Eastern Roman Empire. Tensions over starvation, high taxes, and corruption led the Goths to rebel across the eastern Balkans. At the Battle of Adrianople in 378, Fritigern defeated Eastern Roman emperor Valens, who was killed in the fighting. In 382, Emperor Theodosius I signed a treaty granting the Visigoths autonomous status as foederati in northern Dacia and Thrace in exchange for military service.

Around 391, Alaric became king of a Visigoth faction and launched invasions into Eastern Roman territory before being defeated in 392 by Theodosius and Flavius Stilicho. In 394, Alaric led Visigoth forces alongside Theodosius at the Battle of the Frigidus against the usurper Eugenius and general Arbogast, where around half the Visigoths present died. On 24 August 410 AD, Alaric led the Visigoths into Rome, initiating a three-day sack that ended on 27 August 410.

What changed after

The sack of Rome marked the first time in almost 800 years that Rome fell to a foreign enemy, though the empire's administrative capital had previously relocated to Mediolanum in 286 and Ravenna in 402. Contemporaries across the region viewed the three-day assault as a major landmark in the collapse of the Western Roman Empire. From Bethlehem, St. Jerome documented the event, writing that the city which had taken the whole world was itself taken.
