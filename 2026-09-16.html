1987 · Montreal, Canada

Signing of the Montreal Protocol

Global delegates agreed to phase out ozone-depleting chemicals in 1987 to halt environmental degradation and safeguard human health.

NASA — Public domain · source
Who
Delegates from 198 ratified parties
What
Agreed to phase out ozone-depleting substances
Where
Montreal, Canada
When
September 16, 1987
What happened

On 16 September 1987, international delegates agreed on the Montreal Protocol on Substances That Deplete the Ozone Layer to control global emissions of ozone-depleting chemicals. The protocol set stepped limits on chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) and halons, categorizing compounds into annexes with specific reduction schedules. For non-Article 5 parties, production and consumption of Group I Annex A CFCs were frozen at 1986 base levels on 1 July 1989, followed by a 75 percent phasedown by 1994 and complete phase-out by 1996.

The agreement included specific provisions for developing nations under Article 5, giving them additional time and establishing a dedicated financial mechanism under Article 10 to assist with compliance. Temporary exemptions were permitted for essential uses, including metered-dose inhalers for asthma treatment and halon fire suppression systems in submarines and aircraft. Decisions were anchored in scientific evaluations, using expert input from the Scientific Assessment Panel created in 1989 and the Technology and Economic Assessment Panel established in 1990.

What changed after

The treaty achieved universal ratification across 197 states and the European Union. Revisions and amendments were subsequently adopted in London in 1990, Copenhagen in 1992, Vienna in 1995, Montreal in 1997 and 2007, Beijing in 1999, Kigali in 2016, and Quito in 2018. The phase-out of less damaging HCFCs commenced in 1996, aiming for full elimination by 2030.

Atmospheric measurements indicate that the ozone hole over Antarctica is recovering. Environmental projections estimate that global ozone levels will return to 1980 baseline levels by 2040 across most of the world and by 2066 over Antarctica.

2022 · Let Yet Kone, Myanmar

Let Yet Kone Massacre

Burmese military forces launched air and ground attacks on a monastic school in Sagaing Region, killing 13 villagers and children.

Who
Myanmar Air Force and Army infantry troops
What
Attacked a school killing 13 civilians
Where
Let Yet Kone, Sagaing Region, Myanmar
When
September 16, 2022, around 1:00 PM
What happened

Around 1:00 PM on 16 September 2022, two Russian-made Mi-35 helicopters carried out an airstrike against a monastic school near Maha Dhammayanthi Monastery in Let Yet Kone, where 250 students were attending class. Russian-made Mi-17 helicopters landed ground infantry from the 368th Light Infantry Battalion under the 10th Military Operations Command. Military forces shelled the school grounds with rockets and machine gun fire for 45 minutes while students attempted to shelter.

Ground troops entered the school complex following the bombardment, gathering students and teachers while clearing bodies from the premises. The attack killed 13 civilians, including eight children between the ages of 7 and 16. Soldiers detained two injured teachers, 20 wounded students, and six villagers as hostages, while Russian-made S-5 rockets were later recovered from the site.

What changed after

Following the assault, 50,000 residents on the western bank of the Mu River fled their homes. Army forces transported victims' corpses to Ye-U cemetery, 11 kilometres from the village, where they secretly cremated them. Military authorities forced two surviving schoolchildren to recite scripted statements claiming resistance troops occupied the village before releasing them.

On 30 November 2022, army forces raided 19 villages across Depayin Township and torched 200 homes in Let Yet Kone. The opposition National Unity Government condemned the strike as a war crime and demanded an international ban on sales of jet fuel to Myanmar.

Tanah Melayu / Malaysia
1963 · Sabah, Malaysia

Formation of Malaysia and Integration of Sabah

The Crown Colony of North Borneo joined the new Federation of Malaysia as the state of Sabah in September 1963.

Integrated North Borneo into Malaysia as Sabah
Where
Sabah, Malaysia

What happened

On 16 September 1963, the British Crown Colony of North Borneo officially joined the Federation of Malaysia, taking the name Sabah. The territory had functioned as a British Crown colony since 1946 following the end of the British Military Administration, integrating Labuan into its boundaries on 15 July 1946. Executive and Legislative Councils formed in October 1950 oversaw governance alongside four residencies directed by residents and district officers.

Local governance expanded under the Rural Development Ordinance of 1951, which formed the first local authority in Kota Belud District on 1 January 1952. Urban administration followed through a 1954 ordinance establishing town boards in Jesselton, Sandakan, Tawau, and Labuan. Transportation infrastructure developed after the 1953 Road Traffic Ordinance, which consolidated 503 initial bus operating companies down to 206 by the end of that year.

What changed after

Following integration on 16 September 1963, the office of Governor of the Crown Colony of North Borneo transitioned to Yang di-Pertua Negara, which was renamed Tuan Yang Terutama Yang di-Pertua Negeri Sabah under Enactment No. 17 of 1976. The power to appoint the head of state shifted from the British Crown to the Yang di-Pertuan Agong of Malaysia.

Local administrative oversight shifted from colonial district officers to Malaysian state institutions. Public bus companies established during colonial rule, including the Luen Thung Transit Company, Penampang Union Transport Company, and Tuaran Union Transport Company, continued operating regional transport routes across Sabah.
