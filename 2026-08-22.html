What happened

In 1864, twelve nations adopted the First Geneva Convention to establish binding international legal rules for treating victims of armed conflict. The initial treaty contained ten articles designed to ensure that wounded and sick soldiers out of battle were humanely treated rather than killed, injured, tortured, or subjected to biological experimentation. It extended protection to non-combatants, including medical and religious personnel, as well as civilians assisting the wounded in battle zones.

The agreement mandated the impartial reception and care of all combatants, regardless of their nationality. It established immunity from capture and destruction for medical facilities and transports, while adopting the Red Cross symbol to identify protected persons and equipment. Additionally, the treaty required belligerent parties to record details about dead and wounded soldiers and transmit that information to the opposing side.

What changed after

The original agreement was later expanded from ten to 64 articles to address evolving military technology and combat conditions. Subsequent revisions took place during international conferences in 1906, 1929, and 1949, while Hague Conventions in 1899 and 1907 extended protective rules to naval warfare.

By 1951, the International Committee of the Red Cross directed further updates to align treaty provisions with humanitarian needs during conflict. Currently, 196 countries are party to the four 1949 Geneva Conventions, binding signatories to treat non-combatants, medical staff, and surrendered forces humanely under international law.

What they did

Sellapan Ramanathan was born in Singapore on July 3, 1924, and spent part of his childhood in Muar, Johor. During the Japanese occupation in World War II, he worked as a translator for the Japanese civilian police. After completing secondary studies through a correspondence course with Wolsey Hall, Oxford, he graduated from the University of Malaya in Singapore with a Diploma in Social Studies in 1954. He joined the Singapore Civil Service in 1955, serving in the Labour Ministry before being seconded to the National Trades Union Congress between 1962 and 1966.

Nathan later held senior posts in the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Ministry of Home Affairs, and Security and Intelligence Division. In 1974, during the Laju incident, he accompanied armed terrorists to Kuwait to secure the release of civilian hostages. He served as First Permanent Secretary of the Foreign Ministry from 1979 to 1982, Executive Chairman of The Straits Times Press from 1982 to 1988, High Commissioner to Malaysia from 1988 to 1990, and Ambassador to the United States from 1990 to 1996. He was elected President of Singapore unopposed in 1999 and re-elected in 2005, serving two terms until 2011.

What still stands

Nathan established the President's Challenge charitable initiative in 2000 during his head-of-state tenure. Following his presidency, the Singapore University of Social Sciences named its School of Human Development and Social Services the S R Nathan School of Human Development in 2018. The Government of Singapore awarded him the Order of Temasek First Class in 2013 and accorded him a state funeral in 2016.
