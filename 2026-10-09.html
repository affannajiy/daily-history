What happened

On 9 October 2006 North Korea performed its first nuclear test, detonating a plutonium‑based device underground. The blast made the country the tenth to develop and the eighth to openly test nuclear weapons. Earlier, on 3 October 2006, Pyongyang announced it would soon conduct a test, and the explosion was estimated at less than one kiloton, with some radioactive output detected. United States officials suggested the device may have misfired, and an anonymous embassy official said the yield was smaller than expected; a later scientific paper estimated the yield at 0.48 kilotons. Chinese authorities received a 20‑minute advance notice of the test and sent an emergency alert to the U.S. Embassy in Beijing. President George W. Bush was informed shortly after 10 p.m. (UTC‑5) by National Security Advisor Stephen Hadley that a test was imminent.

What changed after

The detonation placed North Korea among the handful of states that have both developed and openly tested nuclear weapons, making it the tenth country to develop and the eighth to conduct an open test. The advance notice to China triggered an emergency alert to the U.S. Embassy in Beijing, leading President George W. Bush to receive a direct briefing from National Security Advisor Stephen Hadley shortly after 10 p.m. (UTC‑5) on the day of the test, marking a rapid escalation in U.S. awareness and diplomatic response. The test also prompted international monitoring of radioactive emissions, confirming a yield of less than one kiloton and later scientific estimates of 0.48 kilotons, which underscored the modest size of the device and fueled ongoing debate about its success.
